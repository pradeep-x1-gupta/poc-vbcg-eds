Allergy Testing, Requested by Patient

Quick Guide

Background

Patients may request allergy testing for seasonal or non-season, commonly causing nasal congestion or runny nose, sneezing, and itchy or watery eyes. A thorough history and physical exam is essential to distinguish allergic disease from non-allergic disease.

Evaluation

  • For environmental allergy testing (nose, ears, and nasal pollens, house dust mites, molds, as well as cat and dog dander), refer to appropriate allergy panel.
  • For possible food allergies, assess whether symptoms are IgE-mediated (hives, swelling, dyspnea, wheezing, cough, anaphylaxis) or non-IgE-mediated.

Management

  • Treat allergic rhinitis symptoms with nasal steroids and/or second-generation antihistamines the mainstay of therapy.
  • Nasal saline rinses. Avoid triggers when possible.

Referral Guidelines

Refer to Allergy if allergy testing/symptoms despite maximal medical therapy and patient is interested in immunotherapy.

Angioedema, Hereditary

Quick Guide

Background

Hereditary angioedema is a condition that causes recurrent severe swelling of the limbs, face, airway, and intestinal tract. It can occur in response to a stressor or trauma, but is often spontaneous. It is a rare autosomal dominant disorder.

Evaluation

  • Often can be diagnosed clinically based on typical symptoms and family history; may be confirmed by lab testing to be ordered by Allergy.

Management

  • Refer known or suspected cases to Allergy.

Referral Guidelines

Refer to Allergy all cases of hereditary angioedema.

Asthma

Quick Guide

See Asthma page in the Pulmonology Value-based Care Guide.

Referral Guidelines

Consider referral to Allergy for asthma or if regional potential if you have difficulty confirming the diagnosis, or for poorly controlled asthma:

  • Allergy evaluation or immunotherapy.
  • Refractory asthma needing biologics.
  • Severe asthma that requires treatment beyond Step 4 therapy.
  • Persistent uncontrolled asthma or frequent exacerbations after 3–6 months of treatment.
  • Required more than two bursts of oral corticosteroids in 1 year or hospitalization for asthma.
  • Uncertain diagnosis or if additional testing is needed (e.g. spirometry, methacholine challenge).
  • Other conditions that complicate asthma or its diagnosis (e.g. sinusitis, nasal polyps, aspergillosis, severe rhinitis, vocal cord dysfunction, GERD).

Patient Communication

See Asthma Care Education / Asthma Management in Patient Communication.

Chronic Cough, Pediatric

Quick Guide

Background

Chronic cough is defined as cough > 4 weeks in children. The most common etiologies are post-viral cough, subacute/chronic wet cough (dry, per airway cough) syndrome, GERD, and asthma.

Evaluation

  • Careful history, and exam to identify most likely underlying cause as above.
  • No chest X-ray or other tools required prior to referral; can consider CXR if chronically indicated to rule out other causes.
  • At least one Primary Care visit is expected prior to referral.

Management

  • Treat most likely underlying cause identified (upper airway cough syndrome, GERD, allergic cough, asthma) for at least one month.
  • Refer to Allergy if cough not persistent > 8 weeks or no clear cause.

Referral Guidelines

Refer to Pulmonology.

Chronic Cough, Adult

Quick Guide

Background

Chronic cough is defined as a cough lasting > 8 weeks. Acute/subacute cough typically lasts less than 8 weeks and self-resolves. The differential for chronic cough is broad and can include post-viral/post-infectious, GERD, upper airway cough syndrome, ACE inhibitor use, and asthma.

Evaluation

  • Thorough history and exam; rule out red flags like hemoptysis, unexplained weight loss, or significant shortness of breath.
  • Review medications, ACE inhibitors as a common cause of chronic cough.
  • CXR to evaluate for other causes; spirometry when indicated.

Management

  • Any identified contribution should be treated > 4 weeks and then reassessed. Consider empiric treatment with PPI and/or nasal steroid for 4–8 weeks prior to referral.
  • Tobacco cessation (or Ask-a-Doc, telecare) & Consultation support.
  • ACE inhibitor: stop or substitute with ARB.

Referral Guidelines

Refer to Pulmonology, or appropriate based on suspected cause. Consider referral to Allergy/Immunology if the following:

  • Persistent cough > a non-obvious cause when observational testing/evaluation and trials of treatment are unsuccessful.
  • Chest imaging suggestive of non-obstruction.
  • Chest imaging c/w ILD or bronchiectasis.
  • Any red flags.
  • Progressive dyspnea or vocal change.
  • Symptoms (fever, night sweats, weight loss).
  • Recurrent pneumonia.

Drug Allergy

Quick Guide

Background

Drug allergies are common and often account for. Most reactions are non-IgE-mediated intolerances, but some can be true allergies (hives, angioedema, dyspnea, anaphylaxis).

Evaluation

  • Assess timing of symptoms relative to onset of medication use.
  • Assess for systemic symptoms including fever, joint pain, rash — concerning for a more serious response.

Management

  • Stop offending drug as soon as potential allergy is identified and able to patient's allergy list in medical record.
  • Most drug rashes do not require Allergy referral, but if there is a need for ongoing therapy or if drug affect future treatment options, consider referral.

Referral Guidelines

Refer to Allergy, or Refer to Dermatology if patient needs desensitization visit or local reactions testing.

Testing for reactions to other medications is limited, but Allergy can review reactions and discuss options with patients. Ask-A-Doc Allergy / Immunology if you are not sure about appropriate referral.

Eosinophilic Esophagitis

Quick Guide

Background

Eosinophilic esophagitis (EoE) is a chronic immune condition which involves eosinophilic damage to the esophagus in response to foods, environmental triggers, and other allergens. Presents with symptoms such as dysphagia, food impaction (food gets stuck), and abdominal pain in children.

Evaluation

  • EoE is diagnosed based on upper endoscopy.

Management

  • Treatment per Gastroenterology.

Referral Guidelines

Patients with EoE do not need referral to Allergy as they are typically co-managed by Gastroenterology.

For a low-patient with established EoE diagnosis, refer to Allergy and Gastroenterology for treatment.

Food Allergy

Quick Guide

Background

Food allergies that are IgE-mediated (hives, swelling, dyspnea, wheezing, cough, nausea/vomiting, anaphylaxis) manifest within 2 hours of food ingestion.

Evaluation

  • Directed history and clinical presentation.
  • Food allergy testing is not indicated for symptoms such as gluten intolerance, headaches, or GI symptoms.

Management

  • Recommend patients keep a food diary and try an elimination diet with the food of concern.
  • Prescribe epinephrine autoinjector.
  • Consider referral to Clinical Nutrition.

Referral Guidelines

Refer to Allergy for IgE-mediated food allergies.

Immune Deficiency

Quick Guide

Background

Immunodeficiencies can affect one specific component of the immune system or the whole. Consider a diagnosis of immune deficiency in patients with recurrent, severe, or unusual types of infections, family history of immunodeficiency, or the appearance of two or more autoimmune diseases.

Evaluation

  • No labs/studies are required prior to referral for immunodeficiency.
  • Consider Ask-A-Doc Allergy if you are unsure about ordering labs for suspected immune deficiency.

Management

  • Immunodeficiencies are managed by Allergy/Immunology.

Referral Guidelines

Refer to Allergy for non-HIV immunodeficiency.

HIV should be referred to Infectious Disease.

Latex Allergy

Quick Guide

Background

Latex allergy is a hypersensitivity reaction to natural rubber latex proteins. Immediate reactions can include urticaria, angioedema, rhinitis, conjunctivitis, asthma, and anaphylaxis. Those may occur within minutes of exposure through skin contact, mucous membrane contact, or inhalation.

Evaluation

  • Careful exposure history and physical exam.

Management

  • Avoidance of latex.
  • In contact dermatitis is more likely, consider Ask-A-Doc Dermatology.

Referral Guidelines

For questionable latex allergy, refer to Allergy since patient on latex contact dermatitis.

If contact dermatitis, consider Ask-A-Doc Dermatology.

Rash

Quick Guide

Background

Rashes are very common and may be due to many different causes: most rashes are not caused by allergies. Chronic urticaria (hives), pruritic urticaria with possible food allergy, and possible drug reactions may be more likely than food allergy.

Evaluation

  • Provider must have seen the rash at least once prior to referral to confirm that rash is hives, eczema, or a possible drug reaction.
  • Establish before drug reaction is more likely.

Management

  • Treat presumptive cause and follow-up.

Referral Guidelines

Most rashes do not need Allergy referral. Please do not refer acute hives (< 6 weeks).

  • High likelihood that rash is related to medications for which there is urgent/ongoing management, need for treatment.
  • Eczema is child under 2 years of age when there is a specific food is worsening patient's eczema.

Ask-A-Doc Dermatology if:

  • Undefined rash.
  • Persistent rash.
  • Questions about rash sensitivity.

Rhinitis

Quick Guide

Background

Rhinitis is characterized by congestion, runny nose, and sneezing. It can be allergic or non-allergic. Allergic rhinitis is triggered by allergens such as pollen, dust mites, or pet dander, while non-allergic rhinitis is triggered by irritants, weather changes, and triggers.

Evaluation

  • Trial of nasal steroid for at least one month (use consistently); add second-generation antihistamine as needed.

Management

  • Can also add non-sedating second-generation antihistamine such as cetirizine, loratadine, or fexofenadine.
  • Nasal saline rinses.
  • Consider allergen avoidance if triggers can be identified.

Referral Guidelines

Refer to Allergy if:

  • Recommended treatment is ineffective.
  • Patient is interested in allergy shots (immunotherapy).
